Tyre Blowout Prevention Dubai — Summer Safety on UAE Roads

July 2026 · By Karim Al-Rashidi
← Back to all articles

Tyre blowouts are not random events. In the UAE, they cluster in July and August for identifiable reasons — road surface temperatures reaching 70°C, sustained highway speeds, and tyres that entered summer with inadequate pressure or reduced tread depth. Understanding the mechanics of a blowout, how to prevent one, and how to handle one if it happens can make the difference between an inconvenience and a fatal accident.

Why Dubai's Roads Are Uniquely Hard on Tyres

Tarmac absorbs solar radiation and reaches surface temperatures significantly above ambient air temperature. In July, UAE road surfaces measure 65–75°C at peak afternoon hours. A tyre rolling across this surface flexes with every rotation — generating its own heat from internal friction in addition to absorbing heat from the road. The combination of ambient heat, road surface heat, and flex heat creates conditions far outside the operating range of a tyre in normal wear.

The Physics of a Blowout

A blowout occurs when the internal air pressure can no longer maintain the structural integrity of the tyre casing. This happens when the rubber compounds soften under extreme heat, weakening the bond between tyre layers, and a sudden failure propagates through the sidewall or tread. The failure is rapid and forceful — the compressed air escapes instantly, causing an immediate loss of vehicle control input.

The Three Main Causes in UAE Summer

1. Underinflation

An underinflated tyre has increased sidewall flex with every revolution. More flex means more internal heat generation. In normal temperatures, a slightly underinflated tyre is a manageable problem. At 70°C road temperatures, it becomes a blowout risk within a single highway journey. The UAE Roads and Transport Authority reports that underinflation is the leading cause of tyre-related incidents in summer months. Check pressure every 3–4 days in July — heat fluctuations change pressure readings meaningfully between checks.

2. Worn Tread Depth

UAE law requires a minimum tread depth of 1.6 mm. However, a tyre at 2 mm of tread depth has significantly less heat-dissipating rubber between the road surface and the carcass than a new tyre. As rubber wears, the remaining compound heats faster and is less able to withstand the thermal stress of sustained highway driving in peak summer. Replace tyres at 3 mm in UAE conditions — do not wait for the legal minimum.

3. Tyre Age and UV Degradation

Every tyre has a manufacture date code moulded into the sidewall — a 4-digit number where the first two digits are the week and the last two are the year (e.g., 2423 = week 24 of 2023). Dubai's intense UV radiation and heat cause rubber oxidation regardless of tread depth. A tyre that looks fine visually may have severely degraded internal structure. Replace any tyre over 5 years old in UAE conditions, even if it still has adequate tread.

Checking Tyre Pressure Correctly in Summer

Always check tyre pressure when the car has been parked for at least 3 hours — before morning driving is ideal. The correct pressure is on the placard in the driver's door jamb, not the maximum pressure on the tyre sidewall. In summer, hot tyres will read 4–8 PSI higher than cold — do not deflate to compensate. If your tyre reads high when hot, the cold reading was correct and the increase is normal thermal expansion.

Visual Inspection — What to Look For

  • Sidewall bulges or bubbles: A visible bulge in the tyre sidewall indicates internal structural damage — replace immediately, do not drive on it
  • Cracking in the sidewall grooves: Fine cracks indicate rubber oxidation from UV and heat — a sign of tyre age
  • Uneven tread wear: Worn edges indicate underinflation; worn centre indicates overinflation; one-sided wear indicates alignment issues
  • Exposed cord or fabric: If you can see white fabric threads through the rubber, the tyre is dangerous and must be replaced immediately
  • Embedded objects: Screws and nails in the tread cause slow punctures — a slow leak in July heat can rapidly become a blowout under load

If a Blowout Happens — The Correct Response

The instinctive response — braking hard and steering away from the blowout — is wrong and causes accidents. The correct procedure:

  1. Grip the steering wheel firmly with both hands — the vehicle will pull strongly toward the blown tyre
  2. Do not brake — maintain or gently accelerate for 1–2 seconds to stabilise the vehicle
  3. Steer straight — resist the pull and maintain your lane
  4. Ease off the accelerator gradually — let the vehicle decelerate naturally
  5. Steer progressively toward the hard shoulder — only once speed has dropped below 60 km/h
  6. Brake gently to a complete stop — apply the brake once you are safely on the shoulder
  7. Switch on hazard lights immediately and stay inside the vehicle behind the safety barrier if available

At highway speeds above 120 km/h, a front tyre blowout is especially dangerous because steering input is directly affected. The correct response gives you the best chance of maintaining control until you can stop safely.
